Two different “ceilings” — and the page used to show only one of them

The column that used to be headed “S6 q97” was read as a career ceiling. It never was one. It is a price today. Three separate numbers now sit on each row, and they answer three different questions.

1 · Price if ceiling lands
board points — an auction price, right now

What he would be worth on the board if his best band level is the one that turns up. It is today’s price with all the weight moved onto the top scenario — and for a young player most of that price is still pedigree, which does not move with the scenario at all. That is why the number stays close to his printed price. It is not the size of a career.

2 · His ceiling, as career value
board points of delivered career — the engine’s own number

The same top scenario, but written out as the whole career the engine reckons that level would deliver. This is the number to hold up against history — not the price. On most young rows it is two to four times the price in column 1, because a price is not a career.

3 · Measured ceiling
board points of delivered career — measured, not modelled

What the top 3% of players like him went on to deliver. Real finished careers, from the same entry route: same pick band and position for national-draft players, same pathway arm for the pickless ones. No model in it at all. The number of careers it is measured from is printed beside it — when that count is small the page prints a bound (≥) instead of pretending to a percentile.

The row that caught this. Nick Madden reads 1,388 in column 1. That is his price if the ceiling lands, and roughly two thirds of it is pedigree that the scenario never touches. His ceiling written as a career is 3,883 (column 2), and the top 3% of PDA · post-draft academy (all positions) entrants actually delivered 1,543 (column 3, from 38 finished careers). Comparing the 1,388 against career outcomes in the thousands was comparing a price to a career — the page was at fault, not the reader.

Two honesties about column 3. It is unconditional: it knows the route he came in by, not how he is playing now — a player already getting games at a good level is not the median of his own entry cell. And the two career columns are the same scale (board points of delivered career) but not the same ruler: one is the engine’s scenario valuation, the other a measured career total. Read the gap between them as a rough bearing, not a decimal.

Read this before you move the dial

The fan covers the PRODUCTION leg only. Under the one law a price is rho(g) × P̂ + [D(c_u)(1−rho) + Φβrho] × v0. The six scenarios live entirely inside P̂. The v0 (pedigree) leg has its own variance and it is not in this emit — the measured ceiling column is the closest thing to it on this page, and it is a population fan, not this player’s.

So a player with few games shows a misleadingly small spread: most of his price is pedigree, and pedigree's fan does not exist yet. A narrow bar on such a row means “not measured”, never “safe”. Every row with rho < 0.3 has its spread cells greyed out for exactly that reason (175 of 804 rows). On 96 of those the production leg is zero outright — the dial cannot move them at all.

Three more honesties. (1) The lens holds each row's realised downstream production factor m fixed across scenarios; some of the layers inside m are not linear in the fan, so this is a first-order reading, not an engine re-run at a different weight vector. (2) 80 rows — overwhelmingly established veterans — have a perfectly flat fan: their remaining career prices the same at every band level, so the dial does nothing to them. That is a real property of the production model, not a bug in this page.

(3) The ceiling is not always the top scenario. The sixth scenario is built on the frozen q97 ceiling, but the v7 age-taper pulls it back toward the band median — so on 341 of 804 rows it prices below scenario five. Those rows are marked ▼ in the “price if ceiling lands” column, and on them pushing λ positive is not the same as betting on the best case: past a point it walks the price back down. Read the six numbers, not the dial's direction.

What the columns mean

The six scenarios
The engine never prices a player at one projected level. b6 builds six of them — five conditional-prior band quantiles plus a frozen ceiling (q97) as the sixth — and the board price is a fixed-weight average across all six.
S1 … S5, and “price if ceiling lands”
What this player would be worth on the board if that scenario were the one that lands. S1 = the floor band; the sixth is the frozen-ceiling scenario. The printed price is exactly the weighted average of these six numbers.
His ceiling, career value
The engine's own career value at that same top scenario — the whole career, not the auction price of it. This is the number that belongs beside the measured column; the price never did.
Measured ceiling
Realized q97 career delivered value of his own entry cell: what the top 3% of players who came in the same way actually delivered. ≥ means the cell is too thin to resolve a 97th percentile and the sample maximum is shown as a bound; the count of finished careers is printed beside every value. Hover the cell for which cell it is.
Board price
The candidate build's printed price, unchanged. At λ = 0 the Price column reproduces it to the point — asserted in this page on load.
rho (ρ)
How much of the price the production estimate carries. ρ near 1 = a career's worth of evidence. ρ near 0 = the price is nearly all pedigree, and the fan is nearly irrelevant.
S6/S1
Ceiling scenario divided by floor scenario — how many times more the top outcome is worth than the bottom one. 1.00 = a flat fan (no measured production variance).
Span
(S6−S1)/S3: the width of the fan measured in median-scenario units. Unstable when the median scenario is near zero — read it beside S6/S1, not instead of it.
Top %
The share of the production estimate that sits in the single top scenario. A high number means the price is being carried by the ceiling.
CV %
Weighted spread of the six career values around their own average — one number for “how wide is this fan”, comparable across players.
The dial (λ)
Weights become WQ6_i × e^(λi), renormalised. λ = 0 is the board, exactly. Push right and the ceiling scenarios take the weight (the optimist's board); pull left and the floor scenarios do (the pessimist's). Ranks re-sort live.
Δ and Rank move
Change in points and in rank against the printed board at λ = 0. This is where the dial earns its keep: who overtakes whom when you decide how much you believe the ceiling.
